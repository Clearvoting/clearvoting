# ClearVote -- Growth Strategy

**Created:** 2026-03-22
**Target Milestone:** November 3, 2026 midterm elections
**North Star:** More people voting informed
**Current State:** Live at ClearVoting.org. 4 states (NY, FL, CA, TX), 80 members, 636 bills with AI summaries. Early traction: LinkedIn launch post hit 44+ reactions, 13 comments, 1 repost (3x previous best).

## Positioning

Using April Dunford's framework to clearly define what ClearVote is before any marketing activity.

### Competitive Alternatives (what people do today)

1. **Nothing.** The vast majority of Americans never look up their representative's voting record. They absorb political information passively through social media, cable news, and conversations. ClearVote's real competition is apathy and information friction.
2. **Google search.** The curious voter Googles their representative's name and lands on a partisan news article, a Wikipedia page, or Congress.gov (confusing). None answer "What has my rep done on issues I care about?" in plain language.
3. **Partisan scorecards.** NRA ratings, Heritage Action scores, League of Conservation Voters grades. These are explicitly framed to support a conclusion. They tell you what to think.
4. **Existing transparency tools.** GovTrack (data-dense, ideology scores), VoteSmart (dated, limited coverage), Congress.gov (government interface, legal jargon). Built for power users, not busy voters.
5. **ProPublica Represent (shut down July 2024).** The most recognized transparency tool for civic tech is gone. No replacement has emerged with comparable reach. This is ClearVote's opening.

### Unique Attributes

- **Party labels hidden by default** -- the only tool that asks "What if you saw the votes first?"
- **AI plain-language bill summaries** with 12 strict neutrality rules (no adjectives, no editorial framing, 7th-8th grade reading level)
- **AI member narrative summaries** -- factual voting pattern descriptions, not ideology scores
- **Person-first design** -- start with YOUR representative, see THEIR record, understand in plain English
- **No scores, no grades, no rankings** -- data only, never tells you what to think

### Value (what this enables for the user)

Form your own opinion about your representatives based on their actual voting record, not their party label, not a campaign ad, not what cable news told you. In 5 minutes, know what your rep has actually done -- clearly, without spin.

### Target Audience

The Busy Informed Citizen: someone who cares about their country but is too busy to research every candidate and bill. They have a job, kids, responsibilities. They want to vote responsibly but the information is either buried, biased, or incomprehensible. They will not download an app, create an account, or read legal jargon.

### Market Category

Government transparency tool for everyday voters. Not a political tool -- a civic literacy tool.

### Positioning Statement

For the American voter who wants to understand what their representative actually does in Congress, ClearVote is the only tool that presents voting records in plain language, without party labels, and without telling you what to think. Unlike GovTrack (overwhelming), VoteSmart (dated), or partisan scorecards (biased by design), ClearVote uses AI with strict neutrality guardrails to translate every vote into everyday English and lets you form your own opinion.

## Target Audience Segments

### Segment 1: The Pre-Election Researcher (Primary)

**Who:** Registered voters who look up candidate information in the weeks before an election. Skews 25-54, college-educated, suburban. Not strongly partisan -- they split tickets or consider individual candidates.

**Behavior:** Googles "[representative name] voting record" or "[representative name] positions" in October. Visits 2-3 sources. Shares findings with spouse/friends. Votes in most elections but feels underprepared.

**Channel mapping:**
- **Google Search (SEO)** -- This is where they start. ClearVote must rank for "[rep name] voting record" searches.
- **Social media shares** -- They share interesting findings with their network. OG cards and share functionality are critical.
- **Local news / civic organizations** -- They trust local sources. Partnerships with League of Women Voters chapters, local libraries, and community organizations.

**Message:** "Before you vote, check the record. 5 minutes. Plain English. No spin."

### Segment 2: The Civic Educator (Secondary)

**Who:** High school government teachers, college professors, librarians, League of Women Voters chapter leaders, community organizers running voter education events. They need a tool they can point people to -- something simple, visual, and unbiased enough to use in non-partisan settings.

**Behavior:** Searching for classroom-ready tools. Shares resources with students, patrons, and community members. Needs something that works on a projector and on students' phones. Values non-partisan framing above all.

**Channel mapping:**
- **Education-focused outreach** -- Direct outreach to civics teacher networks, library systems, LWV chapters.
- **Teacher resource platforms** -- Share the Class, PBS LearningMedia, iCivics network.
- **Conference presentations** -- NCSS (National Council for the Social Studies), state-level civics education conferences.

**Message:** "A non-partisan tool your students can use to see how Congress actually votes. No accounts, no downloads, no bias."

### Segment 3: The News-Triggered Curious (Opportunistic)

**Who:** Someone who just saw a news story about a controversial vote and wants to know how their representative voted. They are not a regular political researcher -- this is a moment-driven behavior triggered by a specific event (a government shutdown, a Supreme Court nomination, a controversial bill).

**Behavior:** Googles the bill or the vote within hours of news coverage. Wants a quick answer. Will leave if the information is hard to find. May share the result on social media if it is surprising or interesting.

**Channel mapping:**
- **SEO for bill names and vote events** -- ClearVote bill pages must rank for trending legislative searches.
- **Social media (rapid response)** -- When a major vote happens, ClearVote posts the breakdown within hours. "Here's how your senators voted on [bill]. No spin. Just the record."
- **Embeddable widgets (future)** -- Let journalists and bloggers embed vote breakdowns in their articles.

**Message:** "Your senator just voted. See the record."

## Growth Thesis

For ClearVote to reach 1,000+ monthly active users by November 2026, the following must be true:

### Assumptions We Are Making

1. **People search for voting records before elections.** Search volume for "[representative name] voting record" spikes 5-10x during election season. Ballotpedia gets 8-10 million monthly visits in election months (vs. 2-3 million in off-months). This demand exists -- the question is whether ClearVote can capture any of it.

2. **SEO is the highest-leverage channel for a civic information tool.** Civic-minded users search Google -- they don't browse Product Hunt or follow civic tech on Twitter. The search "[senator name] voting record" has no dominant, user-friendly result. GovTrack pages rank but are dense. Congress.gov ranks but is unusable for casual voters. ClearVote's plain-language pages, if properly optimized, can rank competitively for long-tail queries.

3. **The hidden-party-labels concept is inherently shareable.** Joseph's LinkedIn post proving this: "Why I Hid Party Labels" resonated because the concept itself is provocative and conversation-starting. When people encounter ClearVote, the party-toggle mechanic creates a reaction worth sharing.

4. **Election season is the forcing function.** ClearVote's traffic will be highly seasonal. The goal is not consistent daily engagement (that is an anti-pattern for civic tools). The goal is to be discoverable, useful, and trustworthy when voters need it -- in the weeks before November 3, 2026.

5. **Trust is the product, and trust is earned slowly.** ClearVote cannot growth-hack its way to credibility. Every interaction must reinforce that this tool is genuinely non-partisan. One perception of bias can undo months of work. Growth strategy must prioritize trust signals over volume.

### What Could Prove Us Wrong

- If voters don't actually search for this information regardless of how accessible it is (the "apathy is terminal" risk)
- If AI-generated summaries are perceived as biased despite strict guardrails (the "AI trust gap" risk)
- If Google's own AI overviews start answering voting record queries directly, eliminating the need for a standalone tool
- If ClearVote cannot rank against established domains (GovTrack, Ballotpedia, Congress.gov) with their massive domain authority

## Channel Strategy

### Primary Channel: Google Search (SEO)

**Why this channel:** Civic tech tools that have scaled (Ballotpedia, GovTrack, ISideWith) all grew primarily through organic search. Ballotpedia receives millions of monthly visits during election season, almost entirely from Google. ISideWith grew to 53+ million quiz takers primarily through search and social sharing of results. The user intent is already there -- people search for their representatives. ClearVote needs to be the answer.

**Specific tactics:**

1. **Optimize member profile pages for "[name] voting record" queries.**
   - Each member page becomes a landing page: `clearvoting.org/member/{bioguide_id}`
   - Title tag: `{Name} Voting Record - {State} {Chamber} | ClearVote`
   - Meta description: `See how {Name} has voted in Congress. Plain-language summaries of every vote. No spin, no scores, no party labels. Updated {date}.`
   - H1: `{Name} - {State} {Chamber} Voting Record`
   - Structured data: `Person` schema with `memberOf: Congress`, `jobTitle`, links to official pages

2. **Optimize bill pages for "[bill name]" and "[bill number]" queries.**
   - Title tag: `{Bill Title} ({Bill Number}) - Plain Language Summary | ClearVote`
   - Meta description: `What does {Bill Title} actually do? Read a plain-language summary. See how senators voted. No jargon, no spin.`
   - Structured data: `LegislativeObject` schema

3. **Create a "state overview" page for each covered state.**
   - URL: `clearvoting.org/state/{state_code}`
   - Targets: "{state} congressional representatives," "{state} senators voting record," "{state} congress members"
   - Shows all members from that state with key stats -- becomes a hub page that links to individual profiles

4. **Internal linking structure.**
   - Every bill page links to the members who voted on it
   - Every member page links to the bills they voted on
   - State pages link to all members and key bills
   - This creates a tight internal link graph that search engines reward

5. **Technical SEO fundamentals.**
   - Server-side rendered pages (FastAPI already does this -- good)
   - Canonical URLs for all pages
   - XML sitemap submitted to Google Search Console
   - robots.txt allowing full crawling
   - Page speed optimization (already fast due to in-memory architecture)
   - Mobile-first responsive design (already done)

6. **Content freshness signals.**
   - "Data last updated" dates on every page (already exists in footer)
   - New bill pages created with each sync
   - Updated vote counts signal fresh content to search engines

**Timeline:** SEO work in April-May. Pages indexed by June. Ranking improvements through summer. Peak traffic in September-October 2026.

**Success metric:** 500+ monthly organic visits by October 2026. Ranking on page 1 for at least 20 "[member name] voting record" queries.

### Secondary Channel: LinkedIn + Thought Leadership

**Why this channel:** Joseph has a proven track record here. The ClearVote launch post (44+ reactions, 13 comments) outperformed everything else 3x. LinkedIn is where civic-minded professionals, educators, and politically engaged people spend time. It is Joseph's strongest distribution channel.

**Specific tactics:**

1. **Monthly "decision posts" (ongoing).**
   Joseph's winning format: "Here's an interesting design/product decision I made building ClearVote, and here's why." These perform because they show genuine thinking, not product marketing. One post per month, each exploring a different aspect:
   - "Why I Hid Party Labels" (done -- performed well)
   - "What happens when AI summarizes a bill that both sides hate?"
   - "I asked AI to summarize 636 bills without using a single adjective"
   - "The hardest design problem in civic tech: making data boring on purpose"
   - "What 80 voting records taught me about how Congress actually works"

2. **Election-season "accountability posts" (September-October).**
   When ClearVote has more states and data, publish specific, factual posts tied to current events:
   - "Your senator just voted on [bill]. Here's what the bill actually does, in plain English." (Links to ClearVote bill page)
   - "How did [state]'s representatives vote this month? The record, without the spin."
   - These are not opinion posts -- they are factual references that demonstrate the product.

3. **Engage civic educators and journalists.**
   When posting, tag relevant people: civics teachers, local journalists covering Congress, non-partisan organizations (LWV, Common Cause). Not spam-tagging -- genuine engagement with people who share the mission.

**Timeline:** 1 post per month April-August. 2 posts per month September-October.

**Success metric:** Each post drives 50+ visits to ClearVoting.org (tracked via UTM parameters). 3+ posts reach 30+ reactions.

## Content Strategy

### Content Pillars

ClearVote's content serves one purpose: build trust and drive awareness so that when election season arrives, people know this tool exists and believe it is genuinely unbiased.

**Pillar 1: "Behind the Build" (LinkedIn)**
The story of building a non-partisan civic tool. Joseph's perspective as a PM, not a political operative. Why certain decisions were made. What the data reveals. This is the pillar that has already proven to work.

Frequency: 1x/month, April through October
Format: 500-800 word LinkedIn posts with a specific insight or decision
Voice: Thoughtful, curious, non-partisan. Never preachy. Never urgent.

**Pillar 2: "The Record" (Social media, election season)**
Factual vote breakdowns tied to current events. When Congress votes on something newsworthy, ClearVote publishes the plain-language breakdown. No commentary -- just the facts and a link.

Frequency: 2-4x/month during September-October, as votes happen
Format: Short social posts with a link to the relevant ClearVote page
Voice: Informational, neutral, concise. Like a news wire, not an opinion column.

**Pillar 3: "Civic Literacy Resources" (Website)**
An About page that explains ClearVote's methodology, AI guardrails, and data sources in plain language. This is not content marketing -- it is trust infrastructure. Educators and journalists need to verify ClearVote's approach before recommending it.

Frequency: Static, updated quarterly
Format: About page, methodology page, FAQ
Voice: Transparent, educational, matter-of-fact.

### What ClearVote Does NOT Publish

- No blog. Blog content requires ongoing effort and competes with the product itself for attention.
- No opinion pieces. ClearVote never takes a position. This is a hard rule.
- No "bill of the week" picks. Curating which bills to highlight introduces editorial judgment.
- No election predictions or analysis. ClearVote shows the record. It does not predict outcomes.
- No partisan engagement. Never respond to or amplify partisan commentary, even if it mentions ClearVote positively.

## Fundraising Strategy

### Why Fundraise

ClearVote's current infrastructure cost is near zero (single-process on Render). The primary costs that require funding are:
- **Claude API credits** for AI summary generation as coverage expands ($50-200/month at scale)
- **Joseph's time** -- ClearVote competes with paid work and Conduction AI for his hours
- **Non-profit incorporation costs** (~$500-1,000 for state filing + IRS Form 1023)
- **Potential future costs:** domain, CDN, monitoring tools, legal review

ClearVote does not need to raise large sums to operate. It needs enough to sustain itself and signal organizational legitimacy for partnerships and grants.

### Incorporation Path: 501(c)(3)

**Why 501(c)(3) over 501(c)(4):**
- 501(c)(3) allows tax-deductible donations -- critical for individual donors and grant eligibility
- ClearVote's work is educational (voter education, government transparency) -- this fits 501(c)(3) purposes
- 501(c)(3) status is a trust signal to educators, libraries, and non-partisan organizations
- 501(c)(4) is for advocacy/lobbying -- ClearVote explicitly does not advocate

**Process and timeline:**
1. **April 2026:** Consult with a nonprofit attorney (many offer free initial consultations for civic projects)
2. **May 2026:** File Articles of Incorporation in New York state (~$75 filing fee)
3. **May-June 2026:** File IRS Form 1023-EZ (if eligible -- for organizations with projected annual gross receipts under $50,000 and total assets under $250,000). Filing fee: $275. Processing time: 2-4 weeks for 1023-EZ.
4. **June-July 2026:** Receive determination letter. ClearVote is officially a 501(c)(3).

If Form 1023 (full application) is required instead of 1023-EZ: $600 filing fee, 3-6 month processing time. Start earlier if this is the case.

### The Purple States Fundraising Narrative

This is ClearVote's most compelling fundraising angle, identified in the PRD:

> "Help us bring transparent voting records to the states where informed voters can have the greatest impact on election outcomes."

**Why it works:**
- Donors who care about democracy are most motivated by competitive elections
- Purple states (AZ, GA, NV, PA, WI, MI, NC) are where every informed voter matters most
- This aligns mission (voter empowerment) with donor motivation (competitive elections) without compromising neutrality
- ClearVote doesn't tell anyone how to vote -- it ensures they can see the record

**How to use it:**
- Phase 2 product expansion targets purple states first -- this is both strategically correct and fundraising-aligned
- Fundraising pitch: "We've proven the model in 4 states. We need $X to expand to the 7 swing states before November."
- Grant applications lead with the purple-state expansion as the specific project being funded

### Anti-Metrics (deliberately NOT tracked)

- **Total page views** -- a voter who finds their answer in 2 clicks is better served than one who browses 10 pages

- **Social media followers** -- vanity metric; what matters is whether people use the tool
- **Newsletter subscribers** -- ClearVote does not need a newsletter; it needs to be findable when needed
- **App downloads** -- ClearVote is web-only by design

## Appendix: Lessons from Comparable Civic Tech Tools

### Ballotpedia
- Grew to 8-10 million monthly election-season visits almost entirely through SEO
- Every elected official has a page. Every ballot measure has a page. Massive long-tail search coverage.
- Lesson for ClearVote: **page-per-entity SEO is the playbook.** Every member, every bill, every state gets its own optimized page.

### ISideWith
- Grew to 53+ million quiz takers through search and social sharing of results
- The quiz format is inherently shareable: "I side 87% with X" gets posted to Facebook
- Lesson for ClearVote: **the hidden-party-labels mechanic is ClearVote's equivalent of the quiz result.** "I evaluated my rep without knowing their party" is a shareable moment. Design for this.

### Vote.org
- Grew through corporate partnerships (Starbucks, Target) that adopted TurboVote for employees
- Data licensing to big tech companies (Google, Facebook) for voting information
- Lesson for ClearVote: **corporate partnerships are premature.** ClearVote needs to prove the model first. But the Vote.org playbook shows that voter information tools can scale through partnerships with organizations that want to offer civic resources to their audiences.

### ProPublica Represent (defunct)
- Was the most recognized transparency tool, built on the Congress API
- Shut down July 2024, creating a recognized gap
- Lesson for ClearVote: **the gap exists and nobody has filled it.** ClearVote should explicitly position as the successor in this space, especially for developers and civic tech builders who relied on ProPublica's data.

### Knight Foundation Research (Scaling Civic Tech)
- Few civic tech organizations have achieved sustainable business models
- Most growth came from for-profit SaaS companies selling to government
- Non-profits in the transparency space struggle with sustainability but can leverage small-dollar donors, grant funding, and data licensing
- Impact measurement is underdeveloped -- funders want clear evidence of impact
- Lesson for ClearVote: **measure and communicate impact rigorously from day one.** Track party toggle usage, return visits, and feedback. These are the data points that grant applications need.

### Key Insight Across All Comparables

Every civic tech tool that scaled did so through **one primary channel matched to user intent**, not through broad multichannel marketing:
- Ballotpedia: SEO
- ISideWith: social sharing of quiz results
- Vote.org: institutional partnerships
- ProPublica: developer community + journalism partnerships

ClearVote's primary channel is SEO. Everything else supports it.
